03About

Jinlan Zhao is a composer, violinist, and interdisciplinary artist working across music technology, interactive systems, and performance. Her practice explores how sound, gesture, technology, and human perception shape one another. Working across composition, interactive installation, instrument design, creative coding, and spatial audio, she uses technology as a medium for musical interaction, building experiences that invite people to notice the world differently.

Trained first as a classical violinist, Jinlan expanded her practice from performance into composition, electronic production, and interactive media. Her recent work includes wearable musical interfaces, machine-learning systems for music, multichannel composition, and interactive installation. She is currently studying Electronic Production and Design at Berklee College of Music, where her work moves between artistic practice and technical experimentation.

Symbol X

Six Unbalanced Equations for Solo Violin and Live Electronics

An 11-minute, six-scene composition built around mathematical symbols exploring women's social dilemmas. Each section reflects a different social pressure — gaze, prejudice, comparison, anxiety, and the search for balance.

Program Notes

Symbol X is structured around six mathematical symbols, each a framed scene addressing a different social context: the gaze, misunderstanding, prejudice, public opinion, comparison, and anxiety.

I. 1/0

Zero cannot be used as a divisor; here, the symbol suggests a kind of looking that should not exist. The gazer hides in the dark, watching through even the smallest gap, while the woman being watched is reduced to a symbol, a label, an object.

II. 8

The digit 8 traces the curves of the female body, suggesting softness and beauty, but also the function imposed upon it. The body may be admired, objectified, or placed in danger because of that admiration. It is both image and problem.

III. x = x+1

The equation has no solution. Anxiety about age, appearance, fertility, and social expectation accumulates on women, forcing them to chase a standard that is always moving further away.

IV. {(1,1)(1,1)(1,1)}

A woman too symmetrically beautiful is said to lack memorable quality; a well-placed flaw makes her likeable. Society names the acceptable deviation “individuality.” This scene examines the shells that women are expected to inhabit: the tolerated and the punished.

V. −∞ = +∞

No matter how much one has, it may feel equivalent to nothing. This scene depicts how women are pushed into comparison and competition, and how such comparison generates unnecessary jealousy and hostility toward other women.

VI. 1+1=2

A brief utopia: a beautiful, equal, clear space where relationships return to balance and recognition. This ending does not resolve reality; it opens a temporary musical possibility in which women are no longer only the objects being watched, but become the ones who redraw the frame.

What the Fingers Hear

Touch-Anchored Audio System for Braille Music Score Navigation

Learning music brings eyes, ears, hands, and mind into dialogue, connecting written notes with instrumental parts and the sounds they produce. For visually impaired musicians learning from Braille scores, making these connections can require moving their hands between the page and a computer's navigation controls, interrupting the flow of reading and listening. What the Fingers Hear transforms a physical Braille score into an interactive listening interface for visually impaired musicians. The system combines computer vision, natural-language understanding, and synchronized multitrack playback, using the reader's fingertip to anchor the musical location and voice commands to shape what they hear. A request such as “Play flute one and cello from here” connects a position on the physical score to a chosen combination of instruments, with spoken clarification supporting conversational refinement. Readers can explore a single part, compare instrumental combinations, and hear the ensemble as they move through the page.
